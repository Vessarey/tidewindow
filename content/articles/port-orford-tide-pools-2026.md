**Port Orford's deepest daylight water of 2026 came in July: −2.46 ft MLLW on Tuesday, July 14 at 6:37 AM (NOAA station 9431647). What's left belongs to the evening. As of October 11, 27 windows with at least 30 minutes of daylight overlap remain in 2026, 22 of them minus tides, and the deepest — November 24–25 and December 23–24 — all bottom out after the roughly 4:47 PM sunset. Christmas Eve's −2.21 ft at 6:14 PM is the deepest low among daylight-overlap windows at any of the four Oregon stations we compute this king season, but the low itself arrives in the dark.**

An earlier version of this guide called December 24 "the deepest daylight water of 2026, nearly half a foot below anything summer managed." That was wrong on both counts, and the corrected comparison is below: July 14's −2.46 ft beats Christmas Eve by a quarter foot, and the half-foot margin only holds against the August run, not against summer. What is true: at [Port Orford](/beaches/or/port-orford-or/) — the southernmost Oregon station we compute, on the far south coast where US 101 bends around a natural deepwater port — the four deepest lows still ahead in 2026 all land in November and December, after sunset — between roughly 40 and 100 minutes past it, per the table below. The [Oregon hub](/guides/best-tide-pools-oregon-2026/) calls the king-season depth chart a staircase that deepens as you go south; Port Orford is the bottom step.

## The shores: Battle Rock, Rocky Point, Agate Beach

**Battle Rock Beach** is the one you can't miss — the wayside park sits right on US 101 in the middle of town. "Battle Rock Park is named for the historic battle that took place at Battle Rock where the Qua-to-mah Native Americans fought Captain William Tichenor's men in 1851," per Travel Oregon; today, per the local chamber, "there is a city-maintained Visitor Center and restroom there for information about the park and the area." "The beach is just a short walk away from the parking lot," notes Oregon Discovery's park guide, and "day-use fees: none" — no gate, no pass, town amenities a block away. The same guide points low-tide visitors toward the port side of the cove, noting that north of the dock "there is an intertidal area with many colorful marine creatures" — sea stars, anemones, and urchins among them — in tide pools.

**Rocky Point**, at the south end of that stretch, is where the rules change. The Redfish Rocks Marine Reserve is "located off the south Oregon coast between Rocky Point and Coal Point, just south of the town of Port Orford," per Oregon's marine reserves program — 2.7 square miles of reserve with harvest restrictions in place since January 1, 2012, and a rule as short as Otter Rock's: "No take of animals or seaweeds. No ocean development." Rocky Point is the reserve's northern boundary, so if you work the rocks down that way, treat the shore as look-only and leave everything where it lives. The program's own suggestion for seeing the reserve is from above: "Visitors can find views overlooking the reserve from nearby parks at Port Orford Heads, Battle Rock, and Humbug Mountain."

**Agate Beach** is the north-of-town counterpart, reached through Tseriadun State Recreation Site at Garrison Lake — "open for day use year round," per Oregon State Parks, which counts "agate collectors" among the regulars and notes "there's a reason the community calls it Agate Beach." It's gravel-and-sand beachcombing ground rather than pool country; a few minutes further north, Paradise Point State Recreation Site earns the same billing — "The beach is great for beachcombing; it's not unusual to find floats and agates here." If the gravel is what you're after, the [agate-hunting guide](/guides/agate-hunting-oregon-coast/) pairs these beaches with the winter windows that expose them.

## What you'll find in the pools

When this guide was first written in July, recent research-grade iNaturalist reports within a few kilometers of the station totaled exactly two. The 60 days before October 11, 2026 look different: 15 research-grade observations across nine taxa, and the list is strikingly nudibranch-heavy — **Spotted Dorid** (*Triopha maculata*, 3 reports), **Clown Dorid** (*Triopha catalinae*, 2), **Monterey Dorid** (*Doris montereyensis*, 2), **Nanaimo Dorid** (*Acanthodoris nanaimoensis*, 2), and **Horned Nudibranch** (*Hermissenda crassicornis*, 2), plus single reports of White-lined Dirona, Noble Dorid, **Gumboot Chiton** (*Cryptochiton stelleri*), and California Mussel. Fifteen reports in two months is still a thin record for a shore beside a marine reserve closed to harvest since 2012 — but it is no longer blank, and the autumn skew toward sea slugs is the kind of detail only people with cameras at low tide produce.

## Winter: the deepest dusk water in Oregon

From late October the deep water moves to dusk, and Port Orford's dusk runs deeper than anywhere else on the Oregon coast. The five lowest lows among daylight-overlap windows of the October 2026–March 2027 king season:

| Date | Low (ft) | Time of low | Daylight in window | Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thu, Dec 24 | −2.21 | 6:14 PM | 69 min | 65 |
| Wed, Dec 23 | −2.07 | 5:26 PM | 113 min | 72 |
| Wed, Nov 25 | −2.05 | 6:25 PM | 53 min | 63 |
| Fri, Jan 22 | −1.92 | 5:56 PM | 103 min | 73 |
| Thu, Jan 21 | −1.89 | 5:12 PM | 152 min | 76 |

*Computed from NOAA station 9431647 predictions; re-verified 2026-10-11. In the December run, the low itself arrives after dark — you work the falling tide in the last of the light.*

The Christmas Eve row is the deepest low among daylight-overlap windows at any Oregon station this king season, but read its daylight column: 69 minutes, all at the front of a window that opens at 3:40 PM, with sunset at 4:48 PM and the −2.21 ft low not arriving until 6:14 — almost an hour and a half after dark. December 23 is the more usable sibling: light to about 4:48 PM against a 5:26 PM low.

The row worth circling is the last one. January 21, 2027 — a −1.89 ft low at 5:12 PM, four minutes before the 5:16 PM sunset — is the only window among the five season-lowest daylight-overlap lows at any of the four Oregon stations that reaches the Great band. The low arrives just before the light runs out: you get real depth and you get to see it, a combination the rest of the coast's king season never quite delivers. These dusk tides ride the same alignments as [king tide season](/guides/king-tides-2026-2027-dates/), when winter's highest highs drain to the year's lowest lows a few hours later.

## How to work the window

For a morning low, use the arrive-by column: be on the rocks about an hour before the low and follow the water out, keeping your exit line in mind as it turns. These winter windows run the other way around: the lit minutes sit at the start, on the falling tide, and in most rows above the low itself arrives after dark — the usable light comes before the low, not around it. These are predictions, not observations; check conditions before you commit to a scramble, especially on a south-coast shore known for winter swell. None of the times in this guide verifies park hours, trail conditions, or how long a safe return takes — those are yours to confirm on the day.

One honesty note: station 9431647 sits at the port itself, in the cove sheltered by the Heads, and its predictions are the standard reference for this stretch of coast. Battle Rock Beach is essentially next door, but Agate Beach and Paradise Point are open shore a couple of miles north, so the exact minute of low water there can differ a little from the table — margin the arrive-by column already builds in.

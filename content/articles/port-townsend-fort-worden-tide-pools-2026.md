**Port Townsend's tide-pool year comes down to one more run: September 5–10, 2026, with the best window on Labor Day itself — Monday, September 7, a −1.11 ft MLLW low at 7:20 AM, computed from NOAA station 9444900 and scored 80/100, Great. The shore sits below +1 ft from about 5:00 to 10:05 AM; arrive by 6:20 AM. After this week, the Sound goes dark: one marginal October morning, then nothing in daylight until spring. A Discover Pass is required to park at Fort Worden.**

If you didn't make July's big run, this holiday weekend is the year's last call. The water won't pull back as far as it did in mid-July — nothing the rest of 2026 will — but a minus tide in morning light on a day off is exactly what a long weekend is for, and the Sound is about to stop offering them.

## The Labor Day run: September 5–10, 2026

Tidewindow computes windows for the Fort Worden beaches from the nearest NOAA prediction station, [Port Townsend (9444900)](/beaches/wa/port-townsend-wa/). Here is the full run, day by day.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sat, Sep 5 | −1.01 | 5:05 AM | 2:50–7:45 AM | 4:05 AM | 60 · Good |
| Sun, Sep 6 | −1.08 | 6:15 AM | 3:55–9:00 AM | 5:15 AM | 74 · Good |
| **Mon, Sep 7 (Labor Day)** | **−1.11** | **7:20 AM** | **5:00–10:05 AM** | **6:20 AM** | **80 · Great** |
| Tue, Sep 8 | −1.01 | 8:18 AM | 6:10–10:50 AM | 7:18 AM | 68 · Good |

*Computed 2026-08-31 from NOAA station 9444900 predictions, MLLW. Scores (0–100) weigh tide depth and daylight together; the [methodology page](/methodology/) shows how.*

The depths across Saturday through Tuesday are nearly identical — all within a tenth of a foot of −1 ft — so daylight, not depth, decides the ranking. Saturday's low lands at 5:05 AM, roughly an hour and a half before sunrise; you'd be working the tail of the window in first light. Sunday's low arrives about twenty minutes before sunup. Monday is the first morning the low itself sits in daylight, about forty minutes after sunrise — which is why Labor Day, at a civilized 7:20 AM, takes the top score even though it beats Sunday's depth by only a few hundredths of a foot. That the deepest, best-lit morning of the run falls on the holiday Monday is a genuine gift of the calendar.

This is also a Puget-Sound-only event: the same new moon pulls the outer coast's lows back before dawn, so Port Townsend and the inland stations get the last usable run of the year to themselves. The [Puget Sound low tide calendar](/guides/puget-sound-low-tide-calendar-2026/) compares this run across the Sound's stations, and the [September calendar for Port Townsend](/beaches/wa/port-townsend-wa/2026-09/) has every window of the month — September still holds 8 daylight windows and 6 daylight minus tides in all.

## After this run: the lights go out

The numbers turn abruptly. October has exactly one daylight minus tide left — Monday, October 5, −0.23 ft at 5:48 AM, but with only about 39 minutes of usable light before-and-around sunrise it scores 27, a Skip. November and December each record 22 lows below +1 ft and **zero** daylight windows: every one of those lows lands after dark. That is not a data gap — it is the Sound's rhythm. The next time low water and daylight overlap here is the winter king-tide season, at dusk rather than dawn: the deepest is −1.64 ft at 7:06 PM on February 17, 2027, a Fair-band window in the last of the evening light. For morning tide pooling, the season reopens in spring.

Set against that, September's −1.11 ft is modest — about 2.4 ft less draw-down than July's deepest. ## Where to go at Fort Worden

The Port Townsend prediction station sits right off [Fort Worden Historical State Park](https://parks.wa.gov/find-parks/state-parks/fort-worden-historical-state-park), and the park is where most people work these tides. The shoreline to explore is the park's north-facing, cobble-and-sand stretch along the Strait of Juan de Fuca — locally, North Beach — which opens up on a deep low; Jefferson County lists the adjoining **North Beach Park** day-use area as "one of the few public access beaches on the north end of the Quimper Peninsula," and it adjoins Fort Worden on the Strait side, so you can start from either lot and walk the same beach. The **Point Wilson** shoreline wraps around to the lighthouse at the park's northeast tip. (Washington State Parks notes that "though Point Wilson is only accessible through the park, it is not located on state parks land" — the lighthouse itself is federal.) Fort Worden also houses a natural history museum and aquarium that are part of the **Port Townsend Marine Science Center**, if you want to put names to what you find.

One practical note before you go: a **Discover Pass is required for vehicle access** to Fort Worden, as at all Washington state parks — $10 for a one-day pass or $45 for the annual, per Washington State Parks. There are no gate hours to thread on the beach itself, so the tide is your only clock.

## What you'll find in the pools

The last two months of community observations logged within a few kilometers of the station still lead with the **Gumboot Chiton** (*Cryptochiton stelleri*) — the largest chiton in the world, a leathery brick-red slab that can reach a foot long — the single most-reported species near the station, just as it was in July. **Nuttall's Cockle** (*Clinocardium nuttallii*) has climbed to a close second in the recent log, with the **Mossy Chiton** (*Mopalia muscosa*) third. Alongside them, observers have logged the **Pacific Oyster**, an **East Pacific Red Octopus** (*Octopus rubescens*), the nudibranchs **Monterey Dorid** and **White-lined Dirona**, the **Shield Limpet**, and two dogwinkles — **Channeled** and **Frilled** (*Nucella canaliculata*, *N. lamellosa*). The Sound's cobble beaches hide as much as any rocky reef — you just have to look under and between.

## How to work the window

Use the arrive-by time in the table, not the low time — the National Park Service's tidepooling guidance puts it plainly: "Be at the tidepool area at least an hour before low tide so that you have plenty of time to explore safely while the water is receding," and "Return no later than an hour after the tide has begun to rise." For Labor Day's 7:20 AM low that means being on the sand by about 6:20 AM.

Footing is the real hazard, not the water depth. NPS notes that reaching the intertidal zone "requires travel over sand, slippery rocks covered with algae, and pools of water with depths of a foot or more," and advises "comfortable shoes that can get wet and still give good traction." And leave the beach as you found it: "Do not disturb tidal pools, marine animals, or other wildlife. If you do handle an organism (again, please don't), return it to the location that you found it." Turn rocks back the way they were lying — the underside is someone's roof.

One more September note: the low arrives near sunrise all week, so bring a layer — and if you're there Saturday or Sunday, a headlamp for the walk out. By Monday you won't need it. One moon, six mornings, and then the season closes — set the alarm for the holiday.

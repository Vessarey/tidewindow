**Pick a daylight minus tide first, then build the day around it. From July 1 through December 31, 2026, the eleven West Coast stations Tidewindow tracks get 953 minus tides; only 548 — 58 percent — overlap daylight by at least 30 minutes. The summer dawn runs have passed. What remains is afternoon water: La Jolla (NOAA station 9410230) has 16 daylight minus tides in December, topped by −1.88 ft at 3:47 PM on December 24, about an hour before sunset.**

Tidepooling is the rare trip where the schedule is genuinely not up to you. The moon picks the day, the sun picks the hour, and your job is reading the intersection. The good news: NOAA publishes harmonic tide predictions years ahead, so the intersection is computable for 2026, for every station, right now. Here's how to use it.

## What is a minus tide, and why plan around one?

US tide heights are measured against MLLW — Mean Lower Low Water, the long-run average of each day's lowest tide. A **minus tide** is any low predicted below 0.0 ft on that scale: lower than a typical day's lowest water, exposing rock and reef that stay submerged the rest of the month. The full definition, and why minus tides cluster around new and full moons, is in [What Is a Minus Tide?](/guides/what-is-a-minus-tide/)

The catch is daylight. Minus tides are indifferent to your headlamp situation: of the 953 minus tides predicted at our West Coast stations from July through December 2026, only 548 have a modeled window with at least 30 minutes of daylight. Our lone East Coast station so far, [Bar Harbor, Maine](/beaches/me/bar-harbor-me/), gets 90 minus tides and 52 daylight ones over the same months, a matching 58 percent. The whole business of this site, tallied station by station in the [Daylight Minus-Tide Index](/data/daylight-minus-tide-index/), is finding the lows you can actually see. And "daylight overlap" means some of the window is lit — not that the low itself lands between sunrise and sunset. Several of this winter's deepest lows don't, as the December numbers below show.

So the planning question is never "when is low tide?" It's "when is a *low enough* tide during daylight, near me, on a day I can go?" That last clause turns out to be the painful one, as the weekend math below shows.

## How low does the tide need to be?

Remember that 0.0 ft is an *average* lowest tide, not an unusual one. A +1.0 ft low still uncovers sand and the top of the barnacle zone; the interesting terrain starts at the zero line and improves fast below it.

Two working rules, both tested against a year of NOAA predictions in [the −0.5 ft rule article](/guides/how-low-does-the-tide-need-to-be-for-tide-pools/):

- **Pacific coast: −0.5 ft or lower** for a trip worth planning around.
- **East Coast: about −1.0 ft**, because the tide range and the datum sit differently there; the full reasoning is in [Why East Coast Tide Pooling Is Different](/guides/east-coast-tide-pooling-different/).

Thresholds matter because they decide which days qualify, and the funnel is steep. Newport logged 29 predicted lows below +1.0 ft in August 2026, nearly one a day, which sounds abundant. But only 17 of those produced a daylight window, and only 13 were daylight *minus* tides. Tighten the filter to the truly deep stuff and August held four mornings: the 11th through the 14th, all below −1.3 ft. Scarcity is the normal condition. (Computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA station 9435380 predictions; raw lows and modeled windows are different counts.)

## How do you read a tide window?

A **window** is the modeled stretch when the water is below the walkable threshold *and* some of it is lit. Every window on Tidewindow carries the same numbers. Here is Newport's best window of the season just ended — kept as a worked example:

| Field | Wednesday, August 12, 2026 — Newport (NOAA 9435380) |
|---|---|
| Window | 4:30 AM – 9:15 AM |
| Predicted low | −1.91 ft at 6:46 AM |
| Sunrise / sunset | 6:15 AM / 8:26 PM |
| Daylight in window | 179 of 285 minutes |
| Score | 88 — Great |

*Computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA station 9435380 predictions; this window has passed.*

Reading it, top to bottom:

**The window edges** are when the tide crosses the threshold going out and coming back. The full span was 285 minutes, but the sun wasn't up for the first 106 of them — sunrise came at 6:15 AM, half an hour before the 6:46 AM low. That's the number to study on any window: where the daylight actually sits relative to the low. A window can qualify with daylight at one end while its low sits in the dark, which is the standard shape of this winter's Oregon lows.

**The low** is the deepest moment, not the whole trip. Water below the threshold on either side of it is what makes the window walkable; how much of that you can use depends on light, the specific beach, and the land manager's rules — none of which a station prediction can verify. (For arrival timing, we quote the National Park Service in the safety section rather than inventing our own rule.)

**The score** (0–100) adds four parts, all public on the [methodology page](/methodology/): depth below the +0.5 ft reference (up to 50 points), daylight overlap (up to 30, with anything under 30 minutes scoring zero), a timing bump from the calendar (+10 for a Saturday, Sunday, or federal holiday, +5 for a Friday), and a fixed regional season-comfort factor (up to 10). The first two come from NOAA predictions and solar geometry; the last two are calendar and regional constants, not tide data. If you're still translating raw tide tables by hand, [How to Read a Tide Table](/guides/how-to-read-a-tide-table/) covers what the charts don't say out loud.

Read the columns and you can see the season migrating: the Northwest got a summer feast and now faces a winter famine, while Southern California's calendar flipped upside down in the fall.

### Puget Sound: big numbers, civilized hours — all spent

The Salish Sea had the friendliest tidepooling schedule in the country. Lows were deep and arrived mid-to-late morning, no headlamp required: [Seattle](/beaches/wa/seattle-wa/) (NOAA 9447130) bottomed at **−3.80 ft at 11:20 AM on July 14**, the deepest daylight water of the Sound's July–December span, with the entire window in daylight. August closed the books in style: Sunday, August 9 scored 98 at Seattle (−1.91 ft at 8:29 AM) and a perfect 100 at Port Townsend (−2.05 ft at 7:38 AM), opening the Sound's last Exceptional run of the year, August 8–13 — day-by-day numbers preserved in the [Puget Sound August 8–13 roundup](/guides/puget-sound-minus-tides-august-8-13-2026/).

Then the door closed hard: Seattle records zero daylight minus tides in October, November, and December. If Puget Sound is your water, the [2026 Puget Sound low tide calendar](/guides/puget-sound-low-tide-calendar-2026/) ranks the summer that was, and the [king tides 2026–27 page](/guides/king-tides-2026-2027-dates/) covers what the Sound does get this winter: extreme highs. We also track [La Push](/beaches/wa/la-push-wa/) on Washington's outer coast. (Computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA stations 9447130 and 9444900 predictions.)

### Oregon: dawn in July, after-dark lows in December

Outer-coast Oregon lows landed around sunrise in summer. The marquee run was **July 13–16**, when all four stations we track ([Garibaldi](/beaches/or/garibaldi-or/), [Newport](/beaches/or/newport-or/), [Charleston](/beaches/or/charleston-or/), and [Port Orford](/beaches/or/port-orford-or/)) posted their deepest daylight low of the July–December span — Newport's −2.52 ft at 7:50 AM on July 15 the deepest of the four. The last dawn run, August 11–14, peaked at −1.91 ft on the 12th. The coast-wide picture is in the [Best Tide Pools in Oregon hub](/guides/best-tide-pools-oregon-2026/); the [full-year Oregon minus tide calendar](/guides/oregon-coast-minus-tide-calendar-2026/) lists windows month by month, and reef-specific dates for Cannon Beach are in the [Haystack Rock windows guide](/guides/haystack-rock-tidepool-windows-2026/).

The season sagged in fall (the four Oregon stations combined dropped from 74 daylight minus tides in July to 18 in September), and winter brings depth without light: Port Orford still gets 9 daylight minus tides in December, the deepest a −2.07 ft low at 5:26 PM on December 23 — but that low lands 38 minutes *after* the 4:48 PM sunset, and the window's 113 daylight minutes all come earlier, on the falling tide. The deepest remaining low anywhere on the coast, Port Orford's −2.21 ft on December 24, is further past sunset still. Late-afternoon falling tides ahead of those lows are classic [agate-hunting](/guides/agate-hunting-oregon-coast/) conditions, and post-storm lows are their own discipline; see [storm beachcombing](/guides/storm-beachcombing-winter-swell/). (Computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA stations 9437540, 9435380, 9432780, and 9431647 predictions.)

### California: the calendar flipped in November

Summer in California was a pre-dawn problem. The deep lows came very early: [Pillar Point](/beaches/ca/pillar-point-ca/) (NOAA 9414131) got its −1.79 ft July low at 5:33 AM, and [La Jolla](/beaches/ca/la-jolla-ca/) (NOAA 9410230) hit −1.51 ft on July 15 at *4:54 AM*, with only 113 minutes of usable light. It kept worsening: on August 13 the La Jolla low arrived at 4:25 AM with 39 daylight minutes, a window in name only. September was the true floor: Monterey, La Jolla, and San Diego recorded zero daylight minus tides that month.

Then the flip. From November, the deep lows migrate to mid-afternoon, and Southern California becomes the best tidepooling in the country while the Northwest goes dark. La Jolla gets 12 daylight minus tides in November and 16 in December. The December pair is the prize: −1.88 ft at 3:47 PM on December 24, about an hour before the 4:48 PM sunset, and −1.72 ft at 4:33 PM on **Christmas Day**, fifteen minutes before sunset — an Exceptional 90, on a holiday, with the low itself still lit. [San Diego](/beaches/ca/san-diego-ca/) (NOAA 9410170) matches it nearly wave for wave; note that Monterey's Christmas low comes later, 56 minutes after its sunset. Station-level date lists live in the [La Jolla guide](/guides/la-jolla-tide-pools-best-dates-2026/), the [Cabrillo National Monument guide](/guides/cabrillo-tide-pools-2026/), the [Pillar Point guide](/guides/pillar-point-tide-pools-2026/), and, for the reserve up the coast from [Monterey](/beaches/ca/monterey-ca/) waters, the [Fitzgerald Marine Reserve guide](/guides/fitzgerald-marine-reserve-tide-pooling-2026/). (Computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA stations 9414131, 9413450, 9410230, and 9410170 predictions.)

## Why do the deepest tides dodge the weekend?

Because orbital mechanics doesn't take meetings. Summer's deepest Pacific lows land near dawn on whatever weekday the moon dictates (the mechanism is in [Why Summer's Lowest Tides Happen at Dawn on the Pacific Coast](/guides/why-summer-lowest-tides-happen-at-dawn-pacific/)), and in 2026 the dice came up cruel. Take the four deepest daylight windows of July–December at each of the ten regional stations above (every tracked West Coast station except La Push): 40 windows. **Only 5 fall on a weekend or holiday, and three of those are Christmas Day.**

| Date (2026) | Station | Low | Time | Sunset | Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sun, Jul 12 | Port Townsend (9444900) | −3.10 ft | 8:47 AM | 9:08 PM | 100 |
| Sun, Jul 12 | Seattle (9447130) | −3.16 ft | 9:40 AM | 9:05 PM | 100 |
| Fri, Dec 25 | La Jolla (9410230) | −1.72 ft | 4:33 PM | 4:48 PM | 90 |
| Fri, Dec 25 | San Diego (9410170) | −1.71 ft | 4:36 PM | 4:49 PM | 89 |
| Fri, Dec 25 | Monterey (9413450) | −1.71 ft | 5:53 PM | 4:57 PM | 79 |

*The only weekend-or-holiday entries among the 40 deepest daylight windows of July–December 2026; computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA predictions for stations 9447130, 9444900, 9437540, 9435380, 9432780, 9431647, 9414131, 9413450, 9410230, and 9410170. Monterey's Christmas low is after its sunset; the window's daylight comes earlier.*

Oregon's count in that table is zero: none of its sixteen deepest windows falls on a weekend or holiday. This is why our score carries a timing bump: for most households, a good Saturday beats a deeper Tuesday, which is exactly how Saturday, July 11 (−2.29 ft at 8:49 AM) outscored July 14's −3.80 ft at Seattle, 100 to 90 — and how Sunday, August 9's −1.91 ft earned a 98 there while the deeper Tuesday after it (−2.63 ft) held at 90. If your crew is school-aged, the weekend windows are collected in [Tidepooling With Kids](/guides/tidepooling-with-kids/).

The hour-of-day pattern is just as lopsided as the day-of-week pattern. Across all 548 daylight minus tides on the July–December 2026 West Coast, the most common hour for the low is 6 AM (58 of them), and 325 of the 548 (59 percent) hit before 1 PM. The afternoon consolation peak, 51 lows in the 5 PM hour, belongs to fall and winter. Set the alarm; that's the price of admission. (Computed 2026-11-03 from NOAA predictions across the eleven West Coast stations.) And when the November–January perigean cycle produces the extreme highs *and* the rare daylight extreme lows, the dates are on the [king tides 2026–27 page](/guides/king-tides-2026-2027-dates/).

## Which tool does what?

All four [tools](/tools/) run on the same NOAA predictions and the same scoring:

- **[Tide Window Finder](/tools/tide-window-finder/)** — the daily driver. Pick a station, get the next 30 days of daylight windows, ranked. If you use one thing on this site, use this.
- **[Trip Picker](/tools/trip-picker/)** — for fixed dates. Give it your vacation dates (up to 21 days) and a beach; it ranks the modeled windows in that range and names the top one, even when the honest answer is a weekday dawn that's only a Fair. Nighttime lows can appear in thin ranges, and when nothing in your dates rates well it points to a later Good window if the current predictions hold one.
- **[Year Heatmap](/tools/year-heatmap/)** — for choosing the dates in the first place. All 365 days at a station, colored by window quality. The July 13–16 band glows at every Northwest station; at La Jolla the glow sits in December. Book travel from this view, then confirm with the Finder.
- **[Golden Hour](/tools/golden-hour/)** — for photographers. It lists windows that sit within 90 minutes of sunrise or sunset and still have at least 30 minutes of daylight, with the sun's bearing at the low. The low itself can land before sunrise or after sunset, so read the sun columns. Winter afternoon lows like La Jolla's 4:33 PM on December 25 are exactly the overlap it hunts; selected 2026 overlaps are written up in [Golden Hour at Low Tide](/guides/golden-hour-low-tide-photography-calendar-2026/).

Beachcombers get their own timing logic (minus tides plus winter swell) in [Best Time for Sea Glass Hunting](/guides/best-time-sea-glass-hunting/).

## What do the rangers want you to know?

Tidewindow computes predictions; it doesn't stand on the beach with you. For safety and arrival timing, we defer entirely to the people who do. The following is quoted from National Park Service pages for the Olympic coast and Redwood's Enderts Beach, linked in the sources — check the page for the beach you're visiting:

- **Timing:** "Plan to arrive at the beach trailhead at least 30 minutes before the lowest tide." — NPS, [Tidepooling on the Olympic Coast](https://www.nps.gov/thingstodo/tidepooling-on-the-olympic-coast.htm)
- **The returning water:** "Watch closely for the returning tide and 'sneaker waves.'" — NPS, Olympic Coast. Redwood's Enderts Beach page puts the standing rule in six words: even at low tide, "never turn your back on the ocean."
- **Footing:** "Algae and seaweed make the surface rocks extremely slippery. Use caution and test rocks before committing to stepping on new surfaces." — NPS, Olympic Coast
- **Preparation:** "Always carry a tide table, topographic map, and keep track of the time whenever hiking along Olympic's coast." And note that predictions are not guarantees: "Strong winds or storms can significantly elevate tides and create hazardous conditions, particularly in the fall, winter, and spring." — NPS, [Tides and Your Safety](https://www.nps.gov/olym/planyourvisit/tides-and-your-safety.htm)
- **The animals:** "Rough or excessive handling hurts animals. Never force an animal off its spot, you may tear off its feet, or squeeze its organs." — NPS, Olympic Coast

Check the managing park's own page before you go; access, closures, and local rules vary, and they change.

## The whole plan, in five steps

1. **Pick the season for your coast.** Northwest: July–August mornings (done for 2026). Southern California: November–January afternoons, live now. Use the [Year Heatmap](/tools/year-heatmap/) to see it at a glance.
2. **Pick the day.** [Tide Window Finder](/tools/tide-window-finder/) for the next 30 days, or [Trip Picker](/tools/trip-picker/) if your dates are fixed. Expect the deepest tides on weekdays; only 5 of the 40 deepest July–December daylight windows are weekend-or-holiday days.
3. **Check where the daylight sits.** Compare the low's time against sunrise and sunset — a window can qualify on daylight overlap while its low lands in the dark, which is most of what Oregon's December offers.
4. **Read the park page, then the sky.** Arrival guidance, rules, and closures come from the land manager; a weather check the night before matters because, as the NPS notes above, storms can push real water levels well above the prediction.
5. **Treat the prediction as a prediction.** NOAA's numbers are a model of the astronomy, not a report from the beach. Re-check your window the night before, and if the ocean disagrees with the table, believe the ocean.

That's the whole method. The moon publishes its schedule years in advance; NOAA turns it into numbers; we turn the numbers into hours. If you'd rather have the hours come to you, the [Minus Tide Alert](/newsletter/) sends one email a week with your coast's next windows — computed, never padded.

**Seattle's tidepool season closes this week. The last daylight minus tides of 2026 at NOAA station 9447130 land Tuesday–Thursday, September 8–10 — deepest on Tuesday the 8th at −1.02 ft MLLW, low at 9:12 AM, arrive by 8:12 AM. The place to spend them is Constellation Park on the south side of Alki Point, where the Seattle Aquarium says pooling is best below 2 feet. After Thursday, no daylight minus tide returns here until 2027.**

Puget Sound is generous with daylight low water in summer and stingy the rest of the year. By mid-September the deep lows start sliding into the night, and from October through December every minus tide at the Seattle station happens in darkness. This is the last call.

These are modest lows next to July's monsters, but they clear the Seattle Aquarium's "below 2 feet" tide-pooling bar — and its "less than 1 foot" eelgrass bar — comfortably, and they land at very civilized mid-morning hours. ## Where to go: Constellation Park and Alki

The best pools in Seattle proper are at **Constellation Park**, also signed as **Charles Richey Sr. Viewpoint**, on the south-facing side of Alki Point at 3521 Beach Drive SW. The Seattle Aquarium's beach guide describes its "two large rock lines where you can find sea stars, anemones, barnacles, crabs and algae," and calls it "a great location to take any age group tide pooling because there is lots of room to explore the intertidal." The parks page notes the shoreline here also takes in a small marine reserve at the end of 63rd Ave SW.

Two practical notes the Aquarium is explicit about: **"There is no parking lot at this location"** — free street parking only, so mind the driveways — and **"There are no public restrooms at this park,"** the nearest being back at Alki Beach about a 7-minute drive north. Alki Beach itself, on the north side of the point, is the sandier, more developed side with restrooms and food, and works as a family basecamp on the same tides.

At the north end of the city, **Golden Gardens** in Ballard is the sandy-shore alternative on this station — gentler footing, easier for small kids. For the rockiest pools and the widest exposed rock, though, Constellation Park is the one worth the drive.

## The Puget Sound advantage: midday lows

If you have read our [dawn-lows guide](/guides/why-summer-lowest-tides-happen-at-dawn-pacific/), you know the open Pacific coast forces its best summer tidepoolers out of bed before sunrise. Puget Sound does not. The Sound's tide is offset from the outer coast, so its deepest daylight lows land in late morning and midday — this final run comes at 9:12, 10:04, and 10:51 AM. That is the quiet luxury of tidepooling in the city: you can have coffee first.

## When do the low tides come back?

Not this year. October has a couple of shallow daylight windows but zero daylight minus tides; in November and December the Seattle station has no daylight low-tide windows at all — the deep lows all run at night. What the dark months bring instead is [king tide season](/guides/king-tides-washington-2027/): the winter's highest morning highs here top out at 13.09 ft MLLW on January 25, 2027, with the Christmas-week peak just behind at 13.01 ft on December 26.

The daylight lows return in 2027 on the spring schedule, which in Puget Sound means afternoons:

- **First daylight minus tide:** Monday, February 15, 2027 — −0.47 ft at 5:52 PM, right at dusk. A technicality more than an outing.
- **First workable runs:** mid-March 2027, with Good-rated afternoon windows March 13–14 (−0.47 ft and −0.55 ft, lows at 2:18 and 4:12 PM).
- **First Exceptional windows:** the weekend of April 10–11, 2027 — −1.92 ft at 2:02 PM Saturday (score 94) and −1.80 ft at 2:52 PM Sunday (92).
- **The real deep water:** May 7–9, 2027, bottoming at −3.18 ft on Saturday, May 8.

## What you'll find in the pools

The last two months of community observations logged within a few kilometers of the station lean heavily toward sea slugs. The most-reported animal is the **Horned Nudibranch** (*Hermissenda crassicornis*), a small striped sea slug, followed by the **White-lined Dirona** (*Dirona albolineata*) and the **White-and-orange-tipped Nudibranch** (*Antiopella fusca*). Rounding out the list are the **Rough Keyhole Limpet** (*Diodora aspera*), the **Woody Chiton** (*Mopalia lignosa*), the **Monterey Dorid** (*Doris montereyensis*), the **Frilled Dogwinkle** (*Nucella lamellosa*), the swimming **Hooded Nudibranch** (*Melibe leonina*), and the **Lined Chiton** (*Tonicella lineata*). The Aquarium's own beach list adds ochre sea stars, gunnels, hermit crabs, and — on the deepest lows — octopuses tucked inside an old pipe on the beach.

## Beach naturalists: season wrapped

The **Seattle Aquarium Beach Naturalist** program puts trained volunteers on the shore during the summer's biggest lows to, in the Aquarium's words, "educate members of the public on how to explore shorelines while keeping animals and their homes safe." The 2026 season's posted dates ran from May through July — including Golden Gardens and Lincoln Park in West Seattle — so there are no naturalist events scheduled for this September run. If you are bringing kids on one of the last windows, [Tidepooling With Kids](/guides/tidepooling-with-kids/) has the checklist, the rules, and the family-friendly habits the naturalists teach.

## How to work the window

Use the arrive-by time in the table, not the low time — get onto the beach about an hour ahead so you can follow the water out to the lowest rocks as it drops. The footing is the real hazard here: the Aquarium notes Constellation Park "is characterized by large cobble, which is often covered in slippery algae," so step deliberately and test rocks before you commit your weight. Watch the tide turn and start back before it fills in behind you. September's shallower lows also expose less rock than July's did, so the pools that matter are the ones closest to the waterline — all the more reason to be there when it bottoms out.

The other rule is for the animals, not you. Look, lift gently, and put everything back exactly as you found it — every rock you turn is the ceiling of someone's home, and an overturned rock left flipped cooks the life underneath. Leave the creatures where they live. That is the whole ethic the beach naturalists are there to model.

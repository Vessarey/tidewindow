**Pillar Point reef, at the west edge of Half Moon Bay's harbor, has one dawn minus-tide run left in 2026: Tuesday–Friday, August 11–14, per NOAA station 9414131 — deepest on Tuesday (−1.21 ft at 4:29 AM), best balance of depth and light on Wednesday (−1.13 ft at 5:12 AM, arrive by 4:12). The year's best remaining window is Wednesday, December 23: −1.86 ft at 4:22 PM. Park on West Point Avenue; the reef is a flat walk around the point.**

## What's left of the dawn season?

One run. The pattern that made July the marquee month closes with four mornings, August 11 through 14, and the best-8 table for the next 60 days shows the whole year pivoting around them:

*Best 8 windows of the next 60 days, computed 2026-08-10 from NOAA station 9414131 predictions. Daylight minutes counts only the lit portion of each window.*

Read the table in halves and you can watch the season turn: the August rows are all dawn, the September rows all dusk, and nothing in between clears the bar. The run itself has the usual shape — each low arrives about 40 minutes later than the day before, daylight climbing from 52 lit minutes on Tuesday to 135 by Friday while the depth fades from −1.21 to −0.37 ft. Tuesday is deepest but mostly dark; Wednesday the 12th is the pick, trading 0.08 ft of depth for 34 more lit minutes. Thursday's 5:53 AM low puts the sun at azimuth 66°, low over the hills to the east-northeast — nearly the same light angle July's twin peaks had.

Honesty requires the comparison: this run is a class below July's. The July 14 window hit −1.79 ft and scored 82; nothing here cracks 56, so every row wears the Fair band. It is still the last call for morning tidepooling — these four are the year's final morning minus tides with at least 30 minutes of light (two late-August lows, on the 26th and 27th, do slip below zero around 5 AM, but with just 10 and 29 lit minutes they score zero). Weekend hunters get Saturday the 15th, back above zero at +0.23 ft, or the dusk pair of September 26–27. Day-by-day tables for the month are on the [August calendar for this station](/beaches/ca/pillar-point-ca/2026-08/), and the [Tide Window Finder](/tools/tide-window-finder/) keeps the arrive-by times current. The convention here, as everywhere on this site, is to arrive an hour before the predicted low and follow the water out.

## What does the rest of 2026 look like?

Pillar Point's year runs in two seasons — dawn lows in summer, dusk lows in late fall and winter — with a long shoulder in between when the deep lows slide into the dark:

| October | 32 | 13 | 9 | Oct 25 · −0.52 ft · 4:59 PM · 68 |

| December | 25 | 17 | 14 | Dec 23 · −1.86 ft · 4:22 PM · 82 |

*Computed 2026-08-10 from NOAA station 9414131 predictions. August 10 through December totals 42 daylight minus tides. July's row is retired to the record books: 24 lows below +1.0 ft, 17 daylight windows, 14 daylight minus tides, topped by the Jul 14–15 twin peaks at −1.79 and −1.63 ft (both scored 82, computed 2026-07-03).*

September is the honest lesson in that table. It posts 31 lows below +1.0 ft — more than any summer month managed — yet only 3 go negative in daylight. The tide table looks generous; the visible reef is not. October is more extreme still: 32 qualifying lows, 9 visible minus tides. Then the pattern flips, the deep lows migrate to late afternoon, and December rebuilds exactly what July had — 14 daylight minus tides and a best score of 82. November stages the dress rehearsal: six consecutive afternoon minus tides November 22–27, deepening to −1.77 ft on Wednesday the 25th, with the 24th's 4:31 PM low taking the month's best score at 79 on 189 lit minutes.

The December peak is a Christmas present, literally. The two lowest daylight tides of the entire year land back to back — −1.90 ft at 5:10 PM on December 24 and −1.86 ft at 4:22 PM on December 23 — and Christmas Day adds a third deep low, −1.72 ft at 5:58 PM. Three straight afternoons below −1.7 ft, all after lunch, no alarm required. Those extremes ride the same alignments as [king tide season](/guides/king-tides-2026-2027-dates/): the cycle that stacks up the winter's highest highs also drains the reef a few hours later. One note on precision: 9414131's windows and scores are computed by interpolating between NOAA's published highs and lows, and the [methodology page](/methodology/) shows every formula involved.

## How do you get onto the reef?

From the harbor side. Park on West Point Avenue at the west end of Pillar Point Harbor in Princeton — the harbor is run by the San Mateo County Harbor District. The closer Tide Pools lot fills up faster than the West Point lot, which sits about 0.3 miles back up the road. From there a famously flat trail — about a mile out, no steep inclines — curves around the point past the outer breakwater toward Maverick's Beach, with the reef exposed at low water. Budget 20 minutes of walking before your arrive-by time, more if the close lot is full.

On safety, the National Park Service's tidepool guidance is the standard. Time your visit to a falling tide — the NPS suggests visiting "approximately two hours before low tide (when the tide is receding)," a more generous cushion than our one-hour arrive-by convention. Wear closed-toe "shoes with good gripping soles," because "rocky areas become slippery with water and algae," and "please keep small children close." San Mateo County Parks adds a line worth carrying onto any reef: "Walk carefully around the tidepools for your own safety and to spare the marine life underfoot" — and stay at least 300 feet from any marine mammal, harbor seals included.

## Is this the same place as Fitzgerald Marine Reserve?

No, and the distinction matters more than the short drive between them suggests. Fitzgerald Marine Reserve is a San Mateo County park up the coast at Moss Beach, inside the Montara State Marine Reserve — more than 3 miles of rocky coastline where, in the state's words, "it is unlawful to injure, damage, take, or possess any living, geological, or cultural marine resource." No shells, no rocks, no exceptions, and no dogs at the tidepools. Pillar Point reef sits beside the separate Pillar Point State Marine Conservation Area, 0.3 miles of shoreline whose only permitted recreational take is Dungeness crab by trap, market squid by dip net, and pelagic finfish by trolling. Nothing that lives in a tidepool is on that list, so the practical rule at both places is identical: leave everything where you found it.

We cover Fitzgerald's own month-by-month windows in a [separate guide](/guides/fitzgerald-marine-reserve-tide-pooling-2026/). The predictions in both articles come from the same NOAA station, 9414131, so a great morning at one is a great morning at the other — the pools are better censused by the iNaturalist counts above than by anyone's bucket.

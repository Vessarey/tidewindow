## King tide schedule: October, November and December 2026

Each row selects the **highest predicted high tide in that calendar month** at one station, using all its high-tide predictions—not just daytime tides or the season's five highest. If two predictions tie, the first is shown. This answers the late-2026 schedule question without letting a December peak hide October and November.

These are station-specific monthly maxima, not a list of every date an official local king-tide program might name. Times are local to the station, including daylight-saving changes. Heights are feet above that station's MLLW datum; they are not flood depths and should not be read as elevations on a common national scale. Predictions are not observations.

## What makes a king tide “king”?

NOAA uses **king tide** as an informal term for exceptionally high tides. It is not a single national holiday on the tidal calendar.

A [spring tide](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html) occurs around a new or full moon, when the sun and moon's effects reinforce one another: highs are higher and lows lower than average. When that alignment occurs near the moon's closest approach to Earth, it is a [perigean spring tide](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/perigean-spring-tide.html). Local tidal patterns, season and weather also matter. The highest predicted tide in a month is not automatically proof of a perigean event.

The high-water question and the tidepooling question therefore need separate tables. NOAA's [monthly high-tide flooding outlook](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/high-tide-flooding/monthly-outlook.html) addresses flooding likelihood. The table below instead compares low-water windows over **October 1, 2026–March 31, 2027**—our comparison period, not an official nationwide king-tide season.

## Puget Sound: high-water dates are not daylight low-water dates

[Seattle](/beaches/wa/seattle-wa/) has a December high of +13.01 ft on December 26 at 7:22 AM. Yet both Seattle and [Port Townsend](/beaches/wa/port-townsend-wa/) have **zero qualifying daylight low-tide windows in November and December 2026**. A striking high-water date does not supply a corresponding daytime low-water outing.

Seattle's lowest qualifying low in the October–March comparison is -0.65 ft on March 25, 2027 at 1:45 PM. Port Townsend's is -1.64 ft on February 17 at 7:06 PM, with just 57 minutes of daylight elsewhere in its window. The [Washington king-tides guide](/guides/king-tides-washington-2027/) separates Puget Sound from the Olympic coast; those stations should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Oregon: depth and daylight tell different stories

The [Oregon king-tides guide](/guides/king-tides-oregon-2027/) compares all four stations. In this October–March selection, Port Orford reaches -2.21 ft on December 24 at 6:14 PM, but its window has 69 daylight minutes. Newport's December 23 example has 60. Those are depth records within the stated selection, not promises of daylight at low water.

That is why the daylight column belongs beside the height column. “Lowest” and “most daylight” are different rankings, and neither is a local-access assessment.

January 21 combines -1.89 ft at 2:48 PM with 311 daylight minutes. December 25 scores higher—90, or Exceptional—even though its -1.72 ft low is shallower. Weekend and holiday factors are part of the score, so the number is not a pure ranking of depth or daylight.

Nor is a holiday score permission to enter a park. For example, [Cabrillo National Monument's posted hours](https://www.nps.gov/cabr/planyourvisit/hours.htm) say the monument is closed on Christmas Day and the tidepool area normally closes at 4:30 PM. Cabrillo uses the separate San Diego station in the table above. A regional tide date cannot override those restrictions. The [California tide-pools guide](/guides/best-tide-pools-california-2026/) compares the stations and their access sources.
